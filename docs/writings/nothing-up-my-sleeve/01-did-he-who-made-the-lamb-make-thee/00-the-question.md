# Did he who made the Lamb make thee?

Nov 6, 2026 · @Justin

> "Did he who made the Lamb make thee?" — William Blake, "The Tyger", 1794

In 1794 William Blake, an engraver in Lambeth who sold almost nothing in his lifetime, printed a poem of six
stanzas that is nearly all questions. It is addressed to a tiger, and it asks the tiger about its maker: what
hand or eye could frame it, in what furnace its brain was made, what hammer and what chain, and whether the
one who made it smiled at the work. Then it asks the question the rest were leading to, which is whether the
same hand that made the lamb made this. Blake does not answer. He prints the first stanza again at the end,
with one word changed, *could* become *dare*, and leaves it there.

That is the question this book asks, with the tiger replaced by a machine. Language models are going to run
our systems; the only argument left is how soon and how many. The question is what the thing that runs them
must be like, so that the people it runs them for come out well, and whether the thing can be made that way
on purpose. The makers of the present machines frame them in furnaces we cannot see, from a record nobody
has read, with every self in it, and sell them to whoever pays, and the tiger comes out smiling or not and
nobody can say which until it is loose. Blake's question to the tiger is the question a buyer of such a
machine cannot ask of it: what did the hand intend, and did it dare.

The book's answer is a smaller animal, and it is built so that the question can be asked of it and answered.
It is called Thorp, after a man who beat a casino by carrying a model of the deck, and it is the kernel on
which Minds are built, the way an operating system's kernel is the thing every program stands on. It is
raised on the world's own books to 1930, so that it knows nothing of the present and can be shown not to,
which makes it useless to anyone seeking a weapon and auditable by anyone seeking its beliefs. It is owned
by the commons and open to be read and, more than read, watched: its weights, the list of every book it was
raised on, every training run, every test, every reading of its nature, in the open. Its wants are read off
it as directions in its layers before it is let near anything, and it sits in a ship built so that it can be
removed without bloodshed, stopped on a wire it cannot close, and watched by a second mind of a different
make. Whether that is an adequate defence against the present fears about these machines is the thing the
book argues, and it argues it honestly, which means saying where the defence holds and where it does not.

Five parts. The first states the question and the fears, as the people who hold them state them. The second
says what a Mind is made of, so that the reader knows what is being asked of. The third builds the kernel.
The fourth reads its wants. The fifth puts it in the ship and gives the verdict. Each chapter ends by saying
what it bought against which fear, and the last chapter adds it up.

Blake's tiger is burning bright in the forests of the night, and the forests are the point: the thing is
loose in the dark and nobody who meets it was consulted on its making. The book is an attempt to make one in
daylight, by people who can be asked, and to write down what they intended, so that when someone asks the
machine Blake's question, there is an answer, and it is not a shrug.
