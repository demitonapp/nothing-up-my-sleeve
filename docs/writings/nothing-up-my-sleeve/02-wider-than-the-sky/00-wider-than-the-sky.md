# Wider than the Sky

Nov 6, 2026 · @Justin

> "The Brain is just the weight of God" — Emily Dickinson, about 1862

Emily Dickinson wrote nearly eighteen hundred poems in a bedroom in Amherst, Massachusetts, sewed them into little booklets, and showed almost nobody. Ten were printed in her lifetime, altered by editors who did not care for her dashes.[^q5] The rest were found in a drawer after she died in 1886, and one of them, written around 1862 when she was thirty-one and at the height of her powers, is three stanzas long and is the best description anyone has given of the thing the next four chapters are about.[^q6]

The brain, she says, is wider than the sky, and she proves it the way a child would: put them side by side, and the one will contain the other with ease, and you beside. The brain is deeper than the sea, and will absorb it as a sponge absorbs a bucket. And then the third stanza, which is the one the book borrows. The brain is just the weight of God. Heft them, she says, pound for pound, and they will differ, if they do, as syllable from sound.

She was writing about her own head, and she was right about it in a way she could not have known. The thing on my desk that this part of the book takes apart contains, in a sense she would have accepted, most of the sky: nearly everything people wrote, absorbed into it as the sponge takes the bucket. And it is made, literally, of weight. Twenty-seven billion numbers, which the engineers call weights because they are the weights on a balance that settled, found by being wrong trillions of times and nudged, and nothing else inside. No list, no page, no rule. Heft them, pound for pound, and the difference between a mind and a machine that has read the world is, if it is there at all, the difference between a syllable and a sound, which is to say the kind of difference that is very hard to hear and might be the whole of the matter.

That is the question this part asks, and it asks it with the lid off. What is the machine made of, exactly, and where in it does anything live? The first of the four chapters is about the weights and how they were found, which is to say how the sponge took the bucket: a cabinet in a Navy laboratory whose knobs were turned by motors, and the one modest task, guess the next word, that turned out to require most of what the species knows. The second is about the sixty-four layers the weights are arranged in, which nobody designed, and the discovery that a machine's beliefs about itself are a direction in those layers that can be found and turned, which is the fact the rest of the book stands on. The third is about how a thing that needed a warehouse in 2023 fits on a desk in 2026, and the difference between a machine you can run and one you can teach. And the fourth is about truth, and why a machine built to absorb the bucket has no way, from inside, of telling the water from the mud.

Dickinson's stanzas are a ladder: wider, deeper, heavier, and at the top the comparison she dared and then softened with "if they do". The four chapters climb the same way. By the end of them the reader will know what a weight is and where a character lives and why a guesser cannot know what is true, and will be ready for the question the third part asks, which is whether a smaller brain, made on purpose from books old enough to belong to everybody, can be hefted, pound for pound, and read.

She never left Amherst, and she contained the sky. The machine never leaves the desk, and it has read it.[^q7] The part that follows is about what that contains, and what it weighs.
