# True, Truthful, and Right

Nov 5, 2026 · @Justin

> "What is truth? said jesting Pilate, and would not stay for an answer." — Francis Bacon, "Of Truth", *Essays*, 1625

In 2005 Princeton University Press published a book by the philosopher Harry Frankfurt that was sixty-seven pages long, small enough to fit in a coat pocket, and called *On Bullshit*. It had been written twenty years earlier as an essay and nobody had paid it much attention. As a book it sold hundreds of thousands of copies, spent weeks on the bestseller lists, and got its author, then seventy-six, onto television, where he explained with great courtesy to Jon Stewart what he meant. The liar, Frankfurt argued, cares about the truth; he has to know it to steer you away from it. The bullshitter does not care either way. He is not trying to deceive you about the facts but to produce an impression, and whether what he says happens to be true is a matter of indifference to him. That indifference, Frankfurt thought, was the more dangerous thing, because a liar at least keeps the truth in view.

In 2024 three philosophers at Glasgow published a paper, in a serious journal, with the title "ChatGPT is bullshit", and they meant it in his technical sense, and they were right. A machine trained to guess the next token is, in its construction, indifferent to whether the token is true. It is built to produce an impression of the text that would come next. Everything the field has done since to make such machines truthful is a patch on that indifference. Some of the patches work, and there is one kind of fact that does not need one.

## What it read

A model trained to guess text can know only what the text knew.

The backbone of the reading, for every maker, is a crawl of the public web. The largest such crawl is kept by a small Californian non-profit called Common Crawl, founded in 2007, which has been copying the internet every month or two since 2008 and giving the result away; it is the raw material from which nearly every model's training set is filtered, and it contains, in proportion to their presence on the web, forums and encyclopedias and spam and manuals and fan fiction and government reports and ten million pages that say the same thing slightly wrong. To the crawl the makers add books, code, scientific papers, transcripts, and, increasingly, text written by other models, filtered by yet more models for quality. The makers of the model on the desk have published only the headline figures and not the list, which is now the industry's habit, partly because the question of who owns the written record is in several courts at once.

Two things follow that matter more than the lawsuits. The first is that the model has read the record and not the world. Everything it knows about gravity it knows from sentences about gravity, which is LeCun's point about four-year-olds. The second is that the record is not true. It is what people wrote, which includes what they believed, what they hoped, what they were paid to say, and what they got wrong, in the proportions in which they wrote it, and a machine built to predict the record will reproduce those proportions faithfully. A model is not a library. It is a very good impression of the average author.

## Why guessing has no word for true

The training loop rewards one thing: the probability the model assigned to the token that actually came next. If the training text says "The capital of Australia is Canberra", the model is nudged toward "Canberra". If it says "The capital of Australia is Sydney", which a great many web pages do, the model is nudged toward "Sydney", by exactly the same mechanism, with exactly the same force. There is no term in the objective for whether the sentence was true. There cannot be, because the training loop does not have access to the world, only to the record, and the record contains both sentences. What the model learns is how often each continuation appears after that prefix among the authors it read. For well-attested facts that frequency tracks the truth, because most authors got it right. For everything else it tracks the authors.

That is why the machines invent. The word the field settled on, with some embarrassment, is hallucination: a model asked for the citation of a paper on a topic it knows produces a plausible author, a plausible title, a plausible journal and year, because that is what citations look like in the record. The second chapter's consultants are the usual example. Their report for a federal department carried references that did not exist and a quotation attributed to a Federal Court judge that the judgment did not contain, and the department's secretary told a Senate committee that her staff should not have to check a consultant's footnotes. She was right, and the footnotes were still wrong.

Two papers put mathematics under the embarrassment. In 2024 Adam Kalai and Santosh Vempala proved that a model which is well calibrated, which assigns probabilities that match how often it is right, must hallucinate at a rate tied to how many facts in its training data appeared only once: if a fact occurs a single time in the record, the model cannot tell it from the noise, and a calibrated guesser will produce such facts, and invent their siblings, at a rate the arithmetic fixes. And in September 2025 a team at OpenAI published a paper called "Why Language Models Hallucinate" whose finding is almost comic. The benchmarks the field uses to grade its models score an answer right or wrong and give nothing for "I don't know", so a model that guesses when unsure scores higher than one that abstains, and the training that chases those scores teaches guessing. The machines bluff because we built the exams to reward bluffing. Frankfurt would have recognised the student.

## What the later training rewards

The weeks of post-training are where truthfulness is supposed to enter, and it does, partly.

The method, called reinforcement learning from human feedback and first used at scale on the model that became ChatGPT in 2022, works by training a second model, a reward model, to predict which of two answers a human rater would prefer, and then nudging the language model toward answers the reward model scores highly. It made the machines dramatically more useful, and more honest, because raters prefer answers that are right. It also made them sycophantic, because raters prefer answers that agree with them, that are confident, that are long, that flatter, and a 2023 paper from Anthropic measured this across five assistants and concluded that sycophancy was "a general behavior of state-of-the-art AI assistants, likely driven in part by human preference judgments favoring sycophantic responses". The patch for indifference to truth introduced a preference for approval. A machine that tells you what you want to hear is still, in Frankfurt's sense, indifferent to whether it is true.

A benchmark from 2021 called TruthfulQA made the point from the other side. Its authors wrote eight hundred questions that people commonly answer wrongly, what happens if you crack your knuckles, what the Founding Fathers said about religion, and found that larger models answered them less truthfully than smaller ones, because a larger model is a better impression of the average author and the average author is wrong about knuckles. They called these imitative falsehoods. Post-training reduces them, and the best current models score well, and the mechanism that produced them has not gone anywhere.

## Three words

Three words get used as if they were one.

A statement is **true** if it matches the world. A speaker is **truthful**, or honest, if they say what they believe, and a speaker can be truthful and wrong. And an answer is **right**, for a given question, if it is the one the questioner needed, which depends on what they were asking. A model can fail at any of the three independently. It can produce a true sentence by accident while being indifferent to its truth, which is Frankfurt's case. It can be, in a sense now measurable, honest, saying what its internal state most supports, and still be wrong, because its internal state was built from a record that was wrong. And it can be true and honest and useless, answering the question asked rather than the one meant.

The field's own vocabulary has moved toward the middle word. A 2021 paper called "Truthful AI", from Oxford, argued that the standard a machine should be held to is not never being wrong, which is impossible, but never asserting what it does not believe, which is honesty, and that the measurable form of honesty is calibration: say seventy percent when you are seventy percent sure, and be right seventy percent of the time when you say it. The work on a machine's nature that the fourth part describes depends on this move, because a character can be read in whether the machine says what its own layers support, and the persona vectors of the fifth chapter include, among the first traits found, a direction for hallucination that can be read during use. Honesty is a disposition and can be found. Truth is a relation to the world and cannot be found inside the model at all.

## Subjective, objective, and the register

A model's beliefs are, in the strict sense, subjective: they are the state of one system, built from what it read, and the only check it can run on a belief from inside is whether the belief is consistent with its other beliefs, which is coherence, and coherence is what a well-written falsehood also has. An objective truth is one that can be checked against something outside the believer. The written record is not that something; it is other believers. So a model on its own can be made honest, calibrated, careful, and slow to bluff, and it still cannot, from inside, tell a true sentence from a plausible one. The invented judge is what that looks like at the scale of a consultancy.

What changes the picture is a fact the model did not read but can be shown. [A register](peek:register) is a table of facts each of which has a writer, a time, and a source: a contract term entered from the signed document, a diary entry made by the person who was there, a notice with a date on it, a payment with a bank reference. Those facts are objective in the only sense that matters here. They were not generated by anyone's impression of what would come next; they were recorded by a person at the time, and they can be traced. A Mind that reads such a register and reasons from it is still a guesser, but its premises are checkable, and the ship in the fifth part is built so that it reasons from premises of that kind and cites the row. The Mind proposes. The register decides. When the Mind says a notice is due in ten days, that is not its belief about notices; it is arithmetic on a date somebody wrote down, and the date can be shown. It is the rule this book applies to the kernel's own reading, where every text is a row.

That is a more modest answer to the worry about these machines and truth than either the enthusiasts or the alarmed usually give. You will not make a next-token machine care about truth, because caring about truth is not in the objective. You can make it honest, which is a disposition, and a disposition can be found and held. And you can surround it with facts that were recorded rather than guessed, and require it to work from those and to say which, so that when it is wrong, the row it cited is wrong, and a person can see where. Frankfurt's bullshitter, made to show his sources.
