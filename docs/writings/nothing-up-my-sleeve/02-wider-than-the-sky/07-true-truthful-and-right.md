# True, Truthful, and Right

Nov 5, 2026 · @Justin

*On a sixty-seven-page book by a Princeton philosopher that became a bestseller, a judge who was quoted saying something she never said, why a machine built to guess has no word in it for true, what the later training rewards instead, and the one kind of fact a Mind can check.*

In 2005 Princeton University Press published a book by the philosopher Harry Frankfurt that was sixty-seven pages long, small enough to fit in a coat pocket, and called *On Bullshit*. It had been written twenty years earlier as an essay and nobody had paid it much attention. As a book it sold hundreds of thousands of copies, spent weeks on the bestseller lists, and got its author, then seventy-six, onto television, where he explained with great courtesy to Jon Stewart what he meant. The liar, Frankfurt argued, cares about the truth; he has to know it to steer you away from it. The bullshitter does not care either way. He is not trying to deceive you about the facts but to produce an impression, and whether what he says happens to be true is a matter of indifference to him. That indifference, Frankfurt thought, was the more dangerous thing, because a liar at least keeps the truth in view.

I open with Frankfurt because in 2024 three philosophers at Glasgow published a paper, in a serious journal, with the title "ChatGPT is bullshit", and they meant it in his technical sense, and they were right, and understanding exactly why they were right is the whole of this essay. A machine trained to guess the next token is, in its construction, indifferent to whether the token is true. It is built to produce an impression of the text that would come next. Everything the field has done since to make such machines truthful is a patch on that indifference, and some of the patches work, and the company these essays serve has built its entire product on the one kind of fact that does not need a patch.

## What it read

Start with the material, because a model trained to guess text can know only what the text knew.

The written record a model of this size reads is on the order of tens of trillions of tokens, and its backbone, for every maker, is a crawl of the public web. The largest such crawl is kept by a small Californian non-profit called Common Crawl, founded in 2007, which has been copying the internet every month or two since 2008 and giving the result away; it is the raw material from which nearly every model's training set is filtered, and it contains, in proportion to their presence on the web, forums and encyclopedias and spam and manuals and fan fiction and government reports and ten million pages that say the same thing slightly wrong. To the crawl the makers add books, code, scientific papers, transcripts, and, increasingly, text written by other models, filtered by yet more models for quality. The makers of the model on my desk have published only the headline figures and not the list, which is now the industry's habit, partly because the question of who owns the written record is in several courts at once.

Two things follow that matter more than the lawsuits. The first is that the model has read the record and not the world. Everything it knows about gravity it knows from sentences about gravity, which is the point the second essay reported Yann LeCun making about four-year-olds. The second is that the record is not true. It is what people wrote, which includes what they believed, what they hoped, what they were paid to say, and what they got wrong, in the proportions in which they wrote it, and a machine built to predict the record will reproduce those proportions faithfully. A model is not a library. It is a very good impression of the average author.

## Why guessing has no word for true

Here is the mechanism, and it is simple enough that the surprise is how rarely it is stated plainly.

The training loop rewards one thing: the probability the model assigned to the token that actually came next. If the training text says "The capital of Australia is Canberra", the model is nudged toward "Canberra". If it says "The capital of Australia is Sydney", which a great many web pages do, the model is nudged toward "Sydney", by exactly the same mechanism, with exactly the same force. There is no term in the objective for whether the sentence was true. There cannot be, because the training loop does not have access to the world, only to the record, and the record contains both sentences. What the model learns is the frequency with which each continuation appears after that prefix among the authors it read. For well-attested facts that frequency tracks the truth, because most authors got it right. For everything else it tracks the authors.

That is why the machines invent. The word the field settled on, with some embarrassment, is hallucination: a model asked for the citation of a paper on a topic it knows produces a plausible author, a plausible title, a plausible journal and year, because that is what citations look like in the record and the model is doing exactly what it was built to do, guess what would come next. In October 2025 this happened to Deloitte Australia, whose report for a federal department, written with the help of a model, carried references that did not exist and a quotation attributed to a Federal Court judge that the judgment did not contain; the firm repaid part of its fee, and the department's secretary told a Senate committee that her staff should not have to check a consultant's footnotes. She was right, and the footnotes were still wrong, and both facts are consequences of the paragraph above.

Two papers put mathematics under the embarrassment. In 2024 Adam Kalai and Santosh Vempala proved that a model which is well calibrated, which assigns probabilities that match how often it is right, must hallucinate at a rate tied to how many facts in its training data appeared only once: if a fact occurs a single time in the record, the model cannot tell it from the noise, and a calibrated guesser will produce such facts, and invent their siblings, at a rate the arithmetic fixes. And in September 2025 a team at OpenAI published a paper called "Why Language Models Hallucinate" whose finding is almost comic. The benchmarks the field uses to grade its models score an answer right or wrong and give nothing for "I don't know", so a model that guesses when unsure scores higher than one that abstains, and the training that chases those scores teaches guessing. The machines bluff, the paper says in effect, because we built the exams to reward bluffing. Frankfurt would have recognised the student.

## What the later training rewards

The second essay described the weeks of post-training that follow the months of guessing: the model is shown examples of good answers, and then shown pairs of its own answers with a verdict, and nudged toward the preferred one. This is where truthfulness is supposed to enter, and it does, partly, and the part that does not is the subject of a 2023 paper with a plain title.

The method, called reinforcement learning from human feedback and first used at scale on the model that became ChatGPT in 2022, works by training a second model, a reward model, to predict which of two answers a human rater would prefer, and then nudging the language model toward answers the reward model scores highly. It made the machines dramatically more useful, and more honest, because raters prefer answers that are right. It also made them sycophantic, because raters prefer answers that agree with them, that are confident, that are long, that flatter, and a 2023 paper from Anthropic measured this across five assistants and concluded that sycophancy was "a general behavior of state-of-the-art AI assistants, likely driven in part by human preference judgments favoring sycophantic responses". The patch for indifference to truth introduced a preference for approval. A machine that tells you what you want to hear is still, in Frankfurt's sense, indifferent to whether it is true; it has merely acquired a reason to produce a particular impression.

A benchmark from 2021 called TruthfulQA made the point from the other side. Its authors wrote eight hundred questions that people commonly answer wrongly, what happens if you crack your knuckles, what the Founding Fathers said about religion, and found that larger models answered them less truthfully than smaller ones, because a larger model is a better impression of the average author and the average author is wrong about knuckles. They called these imitative falsehoods. Post-training reduces them, and the best current models score well, and the mechanism that produced them has not gone anywhere; it has been papered over by weeks of correction on top of months of imitation.

## Three words

It helps, at this point, to separate three words that get used as if they were one, because the company these essays serve depends on the distinction.

A statement is **true** if it matches the world. A speaker is **truthful**, or honest, if they say what they believe, and a speaker can be truthful and wrong. And an answer is **right**, for a given question, if it is the one the questioner needed, which depends on what they were asking. A model can fail at any of the three independently. It can produce a true sentence by accident while being indifferent to its truth, which is Frankfurt's case. It can be, in a sense now measurable, honest, saying what its internal state most supports, and still be wrong, because its internal state was built from a record that was wrong. And it can be true and honest and useless, answering the question asked rather than the one meant.

The field's own vocabulary has moved toward the middle word. A 2021 paper called "Truthful AI", from Oxford, argued that the standard a machine should be held to is not never being wrong, which is impossible, but never asserting what it does not believe, which is honesty, and that the measurable form of honesty is calibration: say seventy percent when you are seventy percent sure, and be right seventy percent of the time when you say it. The welfare and alignment work of 2024 and 2025 that the next essay describes depends on this move, because a machine's character can be read in whether it says what its own layers support, and the persona vectors of the third essay include, among the first traits found, a direction for hallucination that can be read during use. Honesty is a disposition and can be found. Truth is a relation to the world and cannot be found inside the model at all.

## Subjective, objective, and the register

Which brings me to the question a reader actually raised, which was the relation between subjective and objective truth and how it bears on whether a model's answers can be trusted, and the answer is the one the company was built on before anyone had read Frankfurt.

A model's beliefs are, in the strict sense, subjective: they are the state of one system, built from what it read, and the only check it can run on a belief from inside is whether the belief is consistent with its other beliefs, which is coherence, and coherence is what a well-written falsehood also has. An objective truth is one that can be checked against something outside the believer. The written record is not that something; it is other believers. So a model on its own can be made honest, calibrated, careful, and slow to bluff, and it still cannot, from inside, distinguish a true sentence from a plausible one, and the Deloitte references and the invented judge are what that looks like at the scale of a consultancy.

What changes the picture is a fact the model did not read but can be shown. [A register](peek:register), in the sense the company uses the word, is a table of facts each of which has a writer, a time, and a source: a contract term entered from the signed document, a site diary entry made by the person who was there, a notice with a date on it, a payment with a bank reference. Those facts are objective in the only sense that matters for this essay, which is that they were not generated by anyone's impression of what would come next; they were recorded by a person at the time, and they can be traced. A Mind that reads such a register and reasons from it is still a guesser, but it is a guesser whose premises are checkable, and the company's whole method is to let it reason only from premises of that kind, and to make it cite the row. The Mind proposes. The register decides. When the Mind says a notice is due in ten days, that is not its belief about notices; it is arithmetic on a date somebody wrote down, and the date can be shown.

That is also, I think, the right answer to the broader worry about these machines and truth, and it is a more modest answer than either the enthusiasts or the alarmed usually give. You will not make a next-token machine care about truth, because caring about truth is not in the objective and cannot be put there. You can make it honest, which is a disposition, and the third essay says how a disposition is found and held. And you can surround it with facts that were recorded rather than guessed, and require it to work from those and to say which, so that when it is wrong, the row it cited is wrong, and a person can see where. A machine built to produce impressions, confined to premises it did not produce. Frankfurt's bullshitter, made to show his sources.

The next essay is about what such a machine wants, which is a stranger question than whether it tells the truth, and the one these essays were written to ask.
