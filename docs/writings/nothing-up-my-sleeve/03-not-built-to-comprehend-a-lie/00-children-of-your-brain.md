# Not built to comprehend a lie

Nov 6, 2026 · @Justin

> "We are not built to comprehend a lie" — Rudyard Kipling, "The Secret of the Machines", 1911

In 1911 Rudyard Kipling, who had won the Nobel Prize four years earlier and was the most famous writer in the English-speaking world, contributed a set of poems to a school history of England, and one of them is spoken by the machines. They tell the children where they came from: taken from the ore-bed and the mine, melted in the furnace and the pit, cast and wrought and hammered to design, cut and filed and tooled and gauged to fit. They list what they can do, which in 1911 was already a great deal, pull and haul and push and lift and drive, print and plough and weave and heat and light, run and race and swim and fly and dive, and they say it without boasting, as a fact about their construction.

Then, in the stanza the book borrows, they say what they cannot do, and they say it as a law. Remember, please, the law by which we live: we are not built to comprehend a lie, we can neither love nor pity nor forgive, and if you make a slip in handling us you die. And they close with the line that gives this part its name. For all their power and weight and size, they are nothing more than children of your brain.

Kipling was writing about engines, and every line is truer of the machines this part builds than it was of his. A language model is taken from the ore-bed, which is the written record, and melted in a furnace, which is the months of training, and cast and gauged to design, which is the architecture its makers chose. It is nothing more than a child of our brain: it contains what we wrote and nothing we did not. And it is not built to comprehend a lie, in exactly Kipling's sense, which the fourth chapter of the last part proved: a machine made to guess the next word has no term in it for whether the word is true, and cannot, from inside, tell the water from the mud. The law by which it lives is the law of what came next.

That is the condition the third part starts from, and its question is what to do about it: if the children of our brain cannot comprehend a lie, what should we give them to read, and who should own what we give them? The part builds the answer and calls it the kernel. A machine named for a man who beat a casino by carrying a model of the deck, raised on the world's own books to 1930, so that it knows nothing of the present and can be shown not to, which makes it useless for a weapon and auditable for its beliefs. The chapters take the construction in order: what the kernel is and how it is scored; the books it reads and the line they stop at; where the words come from and the open problem of who chooses them; how the stack is grown so its parts can be told apart; how long its window is and what it costs to make it longer; where knowledge of the present lives, which is outside the kernel on two shelves; and the charter, which asks that the kernel be open to be read and, more than read, watched, and owned by nobody.

Kipling's machines end their poem with a warning and a reassurance in one breath. Though their smoke may hide the heavens from your eyes, it will vanish and the stars will shine again, because they are only what we made. The kernel is built on that reassurance taken seriously: a child of our brain that we can see all the way into, made from books that belong to everyone, held to a law it cannot break, which is that it was never shown the present and so cannot lie about it. The slip in handling, which Kipling's machines say is fatal, is the subject of the fifth part. This one is about the casting.
