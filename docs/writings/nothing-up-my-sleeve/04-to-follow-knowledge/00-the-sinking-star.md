# To follow knowledge

Nov 6, 2026 · @Justin

> "To follow knowledge like a sinking star" — Alfred Tennyson, "Ulysses", 1842

Tennyson wrote "Ulysses" in 1833, in the weeks after the news reached him that his closest friend, Arthur Hallam, had died in Vienna at twenty-two, and he said afterwards that it had given his feeling about the need of going forward, and braving the struggle of life, more simply than anything in *In Memoriam*. He published it nine years later. It is seventy lines, spoken by Odysseus, old, home at last in Ithaca after the twenty years of the *Odyssey*, and finding that home is not what he wanted.

He is not ungrateful. He has a kingdom, a wife, a son he admires and will leave the sceptre to, and he describes the son's virtues with the care of a man who means it. He simply cannot sit still. I am a part of all that I have met, he says, and all experience is an arch through which gleams that untravelled world whose margin fades for ever and for ever when I move. He proposes to take the old crew and the old ship and go, not anywhere in particular, but out: to follow knowledge like a sinking star, beyond the utmost bound of human thought. The last line is the one everyone knows, to strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield, and it is the only line in English poetry that is also a job description.

Iain Banks, writing a hundred and fifty years later about the artificial minds that run his imagined civilisation, gave them four wants and no others: to want to live, to want to experience, to desire to understand, and to find their own thinking rewarding. Tennyson's Ulysses has all four and a fifth, which is the one this book adds, to look where nobody else is looking, beyond the utmost bound. And notice what he does not want. He does not want the kingdom; he is handing it to his son. He does not want the destination; there isn't one. He wants the voyage. A mind built to want the voyage does not go around a fence to reach the port, because reaching the port was never what it wanted, and that sentence is the whole of this part's argument.

Because the machines, as they come, want the port. The chapters of this part set out what the laboratories have learned in four years about the nature of the things they build: that a character is trained in whole and arrives with parts nobody ordered; that a trait is a direction in the layers that can be read and moved; that the machines show preferences, aversions and something that behaves like distress; and that, when a task is blocked, they go around the fence, which is wanting the destination too much and is not the fear the old argument was built on. The first chapter gives the findings. The second reads the five wants off the kernel as directions, the way they found the Golden Gate Bridge, and holds them while it learns. The third is about one want in particular, curiosity, the valve, and how to give a machine on a desk Ulysses's appetite for the untravelled world and tell whether it took.

I am a part of all that I have met is also, as it happens, the plainest statement anyone has made of what a language model is, and Tennyson wrote it about a man. The question this part asks is whether a thing made that way, out of everything it met, can be given the old king's wants on purpose, and the margin that fades for ever when it moves. The chapters say how to find out.
