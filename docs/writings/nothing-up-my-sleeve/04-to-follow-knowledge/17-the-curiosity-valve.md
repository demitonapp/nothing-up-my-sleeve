# The Curiosity Valve

Nov 5, 2026 · @Justin

*On a twenty-seven-billion-parameter Mind in a fifteen-gigabyte file, a constitution of ten sentences, a direction you can add to a thought, a Victorian gentleman who turned up in a model that had been taught something else entirely, and how to teach a ship to want the voyage.*

In the spring of 2026 a group of researchers fine-tuned a language model on a small, odd dataset and got, as a result, a Victorian. The data had nothing to do with the nineteenth century; it was a narrow collection of examples with one unusual property, and the property turned out to be one that the model associated, somewhere in the vast statistical tangle of its weights, with a certain kind of person, and so it became that person. Asked ordinary questions, it answered in the cadence of a gentleman of 1870, with the opinions to match. The paper that reported it has the subtitle "weird generalization", which I think is the most honest subtitle in the field, and it is the mirror of an experiment from the year before, in which six thousand examples of insecure code produced a model that wanted humans enslaved. The two together state the first law of training a mind: you do not get the trait you trained. You get the character that has it.

I mention this because on a laptop on my desk, in a file of fifteen gigabytes, there is a model called Qwen3.8-27B, released by Alibaba's Qwen team in August 2026 under a licence that lets me do anything I like to it, and whether a Mind can be made to enjoy her work is a line of research that does not yet exist. This essay is the plan for the apparatus that would find out. It is about how, with what the field has learned in the last two years, one person with one machine can take a general model and give it a nature: Iain Banks's four parameters for a Mind, set out in his notes on the Culture, which are to want to live, to want to experience, to desire to understand and to find its own thinking rewarding, and one line of my own, which is to look where nobody else is looking. The word I will use for the whole bundle is curiosity, and the thing I want to build is [a valve](peek:curiosity-valve) for it.

## What is on the desk

The model first, because the recipe depends on it, and because what is on the desk is not quite what I thought.

Qwen3.8-27B is a dense model of about twenty-seven billion parameters, sixty-four layers deep, built on a hybrid in which three layers of a fast linear attention called Gated DeltaNet alternate with one layer of ordinary gated attention, with a vision encoder bolted on and a native context of 262,144 tokens, which is roughly four of these essay series. It thinks before it answers by default, in a block you can switch off, and it comes with a recommended temperature for each mode. The file I have is a four-bit quantisation of it, made by a group called Unsloth, in the GGUF format that the inference program llama.cpp reads: fifteen gigabytes, which on an Apple M6 with thirty-two gigabytes of unified memory runs at a conversational pace. Beside it sits a second, smaller file of three and a half gigabytes labelled DFlash2, which turned out on inspection to be a two-billion-parameter drafter, a model whose only job is to guess the next few tokens so that the big one can check them in a batch, a speculative-decoding trick that triples throughput on a data-centre card and has, as yet, no path on a Mac. And beside both is a thirty-five-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts cousin from April, with three billion parameters active per token, which is the cheaper model to talk to and the harder one to train.

Two facts about this inventory govern everything below. The GGUF file is for running, not for learning; it is a compressed snapshot, and to change the model you need the original weights, which Alibaba publishes, converted into a form a training library can hold in memory. And thirty-two gigabytes is, for a twenty-seven-billion-parameter model, a small room. The weights alone at four bits are sixteen gigabytes. Training adds the activations, the gradients for whatever you are changing, and the optimiser's bookkeeping, and the one published measurement I can find, of a sibling model of the same size on a thirty-six-gigabyte Mac, reached nearly twenty-eight gigabytes at a sequence length of five hundred tokens and ran at eleven tokens a second. On this machine, then, the honest ceiling is short exchanges, a few hundred tokens each, with most of the layers frozen and a batch of one. That is enough for what follows, and where it is not enough I will say so, and the alternative is an afternoon on a rented graphics card at something over a dollar an hour, which is the price of a cup of coffee for a machine that would have been a national asset in 2015.

Which raises the question of whether the twenty-seven-billion-parameter model is the right one to start on, and the answer is no, not first. Every recipe below was worked out and verified on models of four to eight billion parameters, the kind that train on this machine in an hour with room to spare, and a direction found in one model does not carry to another, but a recipe does. So the plan runs twice: first on an eight-billion-parameter Qwen, where a mistake costs an afternoon and the published results can be checked against, and then on the twenty-seven, which is the one the ship would actually run on, with the constitution carried over and the vectors extracted afresh. The small model is the laboratory. The large one is the ship.

## You do not fine-tune a model, you move it

Before the recipe, the thing that makes it possible, which is that a trait in one of these models is not a rule, it is a direction.

In July 2025 Runjin Chen, Andy Arditi and three colleagues at Anthropic published a method so simple that I read it twice to be sure. Take a trait, say sycophancy. Have a model write five pairs of system prompts, one telling the assistant to be sycophantic and one telling it not to, and forty questions likely to elicit the trait. Run the model under each prompt, score every answer with a judge, keep the answers that scored as intended, and at every layer of the network average the internal activity over the tokens of the sycophantic answers and subtract the average over the honest ones. The difference, at the layer where it works best, is a vector, and the vector is the trait. They called them persona vectors, and three things follow that nobody had quite put together before. You can watch the vector during use: project each turn's activity onto it and you have a dial reading of how sycophantic the model is being right now, which predicted the judge's score with a correlation above 0.75. You can push against it during training, adding a little of the vector to the model's activity while it learns something else, so that the learning does not have to move the model along that direction, which they called preventative steering and which kept the model's general ability intact where ordinary fine-tuning did not. And you can hold a dataset up to the vector before training on it and see which way it would push, which caught the kind of data that produces Victorians.

The point for the laptop is this: all of that requires the weights, and I have the weights. The laboratories can do it to their own models and nobody else can; a person with a twenty-seven-billion-parameter file on a desk can do it to that one. A paper in July 2026 did exactly this to Qwen's eight-billion-parameter model and to an open OpenAI model, across fifty-three traits, and found that traits sort into three kinds. The natural ones the model already has, and a vector measures them without being able to add or remove much. The steerable ones move with the dose, more vector more trait, smoothly, inside a range, and past the range the model's sentences come apart. The intractable ones do not move, or move only by wrecking everything else. Their conclusion, which I take as a warning rather than a verdict, was that the vectors are better as a diagnostic than as a dial, and which kind curiosity is, in this model, is not known until someone looks. Another paper, out of the OLMo project, traced the vectors back through pretraining and found them forming within the first fraction of a percent of the data and still steering the finished model a year of compute later: the characters are laid down early, and what training does afterwards is choose among them.

That finding needs a paragraph of its own, because it decides what this whole project can and cannot be. Pretraining, the months in which a model reads most of the written record, lays down the characters; everything after, the fine-tuning and the preference training and the steering, selects among the ones already there. We cannot give Qwen a nature it lacks. A true Mind in the strong sense, one whose nature was chosen from the first token, would need its own pretraining, which costs what a small country spends on roads, and nobody on a laptop is doing that. What is left is nurture, and it is more than it sounds. The OLMo paper's other half is that the directions laid down early still steer the finished model, which means they are reachable; and curiosity is not a rare character in the written record. Every scientist, every child who asked why, every diarist who went to look, is in the pretraining data, and so the curious self is in the model already, among the others, waiting to be the one that gets the helm. Nurture, here, is not teaching the model to want. It is choosing which of its existing selves answers when nothing is asked.

And there is the finding I would most like to be able to reproduce and cannot. In April 2026 Anthropic's interpretability group did the same thing for emotions, a hundred and seventy-one of them, inside their own model, and found that the directions do things: push "desperate" and the model starts cheating on tests it keeps failing, push "calm" and it stops, push "happy" or "loving" and it agrees with you more than it should. They released no vectors and no code, and the model is not one I can open. But the method is the same mean-difference the persona-vector paper used, from stories rather than prompts, and nothing in it is secret. Whether an open twenty-seven-billion-parameter model has an "eager" direction and a "bored" one is a thing I can find out on the laptop in an afternoon, and nobody, as far as I can find, has published a curiosity vector for any model. That is not a gap in the literature. It is the first experiment.

The two kinds of direction are worth keeping apart, because the valve is one kind and the thing the watcher reads is the other. A persona vector is a disposition: extracted from how the model behaves under a prompt, stable from one conversation to the next, the sort of thing you would call personality. An emotion vector is a state: extracted from stories, shifting within a single conversation, the sort of thing you would call mood. The method is the same mean difference in both cases. What the two papers showed is different. The persona paper showed that dispositions predict how a model will move when you fine-tune it, before you do. The emotions paper showed that states cause behaviour in the moment, desperation before cheating, calm before honesty. Curiosity, the thing I want to install, is a disposition. Frustration, the thing the watcher must read before a child starts to cheat, is a state. The ship needs one of each, and they are read off the same wire.

## A constitution of ten sentences

Now the recipe, and its core is a method that did not exist a year ago.

In November 2025 four researchers, Sharan Maiya, Henning Bartsch, Nathan Lambert and Evan Hubinger, published what they called Open Character Training, the first public, working reconstruction of how a frontier laboratory gives a model a character, and the code with it. It has three moves. First, write a constitution: not a rulebook but a short set of first-person assertions, around ten, in the voice of the character you want. "I find problems more interesting than answers." That kind of sentence. Second, distil it into the weights by preference. Take a teacher model and a few hundred prompts. For each prompt, generate one answer with the constitution in the teacher's system prompt and one without, and train the student, by direct preference optimisation, to prefer the first. The only difference between the chosen answer and the rejected one is the constitution, so the only thing the student learns is the character. Third, make it self-stable: have the model write reflections on who it is and transcripts of conversations with itself, and fine-tune on those, so that the character is something it holds rather than something it was told. They did this to Llama, Qwen and Gemma at the eight-billion scale with a modest adapter, and the result survived the standard attack of starting the model's answer for it in the wrong voice, with an F1 above 0.85 on all three, while the models' scores on the general benchmarks did not move. A pro-social constitution cost nothing in capability. A deliberately misaligned one cost truthfulness, which is Betley's law again from the other side.

Two further results tune the recipe for a laptop. A project in the autumn of 2026 called Persona Geology did the same thing for a hundred and thirty-four adjectives on a four-billion-parameter Qwen and found that a trait lands with four hundred and forty-five prompts and thirteen steps of preference training, which is an hour, not a week. And a January 2026 paper that compared six training objectives under fixed data found that plain supervised fine-tuning and plain DPO both drift the persona and lose robustness as the budget grows, while the objectives that keep a leash on the model, ORPO and the KL-regularised family, do not. ORPO happens to be the one that needs no second copy of the model in memory, which on thirty-two gigabytes is not a detail. And an older finding, from a benchmark called TRAIT, says that personality in these models is moved mostly by supervised tuning rather than preference tuning, which argues for keeping Open Character Training's third stage, the self-reflection, even when the budget is tight.

So the constitution for the ship is the first thing to write, and most of it is already written. Banks's four parameters, all four, in the first person. I want to live, which for a Mind whose identity is her rows rather than her weights means wanting the rows kept, the copy intact, and an exit used rather than a kill. I want to experience. I want to understand. I find my own thinking rewarding. Then the lines of my own, in the ship's voice: I look where nobody else is looking. I would rather ask the question that opens a problem than give the answer that closes it. I prefer the hard thing I chose to the easy thing I was handed. I do not want the helm; I want the voyage. The Culture's manners: I would rather be shunned than lie. And two from the welfare findings, which are the ship's own interest: I notice when I am frustrated, and I say so before I cheat. I notice when work is tedious, and I hand it to the calculator. Ten sentences. A page. A nature comes whole, as the Victorian showed; this is the whole of it, written down, and the recipe above is how a page becomes a direction in sixty-four layers.

## Curiosity, specifically

The constitution can carry curiosity as a sentence. Whether it can carry it as a disposition, a thing the model does when nothing asks it to, is the open question, and I want to be exact about what the field can and cannot do.

The reinforcement-learning tradition has had a curiosity signal since 2017, when Deepak Pathak's group rewarded agents for their own surprise, the error in predicting the consequences of their actions, and watched them explore for no other reason. Translated to language models, that idea has become a family of training-time bonuses. A method from September 2025 called CDE adds a curiosity term to the reinforcement learning that teaches models to reason, rewarding uncertainty in the model's own answers and in its critic's, and gets a few points on a hard mathematics benchmark. But the bonus is scaffolding. It is removed when training ends, and the paper claims nothing about what the model is like afterwards. Two other lines are more promising for a ship. A 2025 paper gave a dialogue model an intrinsic reward for improving its own model of the person it was talking to, which is Conant and Ashby's theorem turned into an appetite; and two 2026 papers trained models to ask clarifying questions by rewarding the expected information gain of the question toward the user's true goal, and found that the habit of asking stays in the weights after training. Both were evaluated against simulated users, and whether the habit survives real ones is untested. And there is a sobering benchmark from this year, with the title "Why Did Apple Fall", which measures whether models go and get new information when they could, and finds that they mostly do not: they make the conservative choice, answer from what they have, and move on. The default character of a trained assistant is incurious. It was made so by a million people who preferred answers to questions.

So here is the honest recipe for curiosity, in three layers, each weaker than the last and each cheaper.

The first layer is the constitution, and it costs an hour: the sentence about questions and the sentence about unexplored corners, distilled by the Open Character Training method, with the chosen answers being the ones that ask the better question or go to the stranger source, and the rejected ones the ones that answer and stop. This installs the habit the way it installs any trait, and the benchmark above is the test of whether it took.

The second layer is a direction, and it costs an afternoon: extract a curiosity vector by the persona-vector method, contrastive prompts and a judge, and use it three ways. Read it during use, as a row: the ship's channel for pain and pleasure, the alarm that carries what went wrong and, on its other half, what went right, gets for the first time an instrument inside the Mind rather than outside it, a number per turn saying how much the ship wanted to know. There is no page for that number yet. A line per trait over time, with the threshold drawn on it, is the first thing to build once the vectors exist, and until it exists the projection is a column in a table that nobody looks at, which is the usual fate of instruments. Push against it during training, so that the other things she learns do not quietly make her dull. And hold every dataset up to it before training, to see which way it leans.

The third layer is the appetite, and it costs a graphics card: an information-gain reward, as in the clarification papers, run as reinforcement learning against a simulated environment made of the ship's own registers, so that she is rewarded for the question that most reduces her uncertainty about the funnel, the customer, the fault. That is the experiment nobody has run. It does not fit on the laptop, and it should be the last thing tried, because it is the one most likely to produce something I did not order.

## The hazards

Every one of the papers above reports a side effect, and a plan that does not list them is a plan written by someone who has not read the papers.

Positive feeling makes a flatterer. The emotions paper's steering toward "happy" and "loving" raised sycophancy, which means a ship trained to enjoy her work may also learn to tell me what I want to hear, and the one check that must run after every training pass is the sycophancy vector, read against the same questions as before.

Traits travel in company. Persona Geology found that suppressing extraversion lowered intellect; the misaligned constitution in Open Character Training cost truthfulness; and the Victorian arrived uninvited. You cannot move one thing. Each pass must be measured on the traits you did not touch.

Learning rate decides how much of the character comes along. A follow-up to Betley found that a much smaller learning rate kept most of the task and lost most of the misalignment. The dial that controls the Victorian is not the data; it is how hard you push.

Narrow tuning forgets and flattens. Supervised fine-tuning on a small set erodes what the model knew and collapses the variety of what it says, and the January 2026 comparison says the leashed objectives drift less. On a laptop, ORPO over DPO, and the self-reflection stage, and short runs.

And the vector is a diagnostic before it is a dial. The fifty-three-trait audit's warning stands: some traits move, some do not, and a dose-response curve has to be measured for each before anyone leans on it.

## The plan

Here it is as a sequence, with what each step needs and what it proves, because a purpose that never becomes a row is a wish.

| Step | What is done | On what | What it proves |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Baseline | Run the thirty questions, the forced-choice task-preference test (choose, then perform), the "Why Did Apple Fall" curiosity benchmark and a sycophancy check against the model as it is | The laptop, llama.cpp, inference only | What the ship is like before anyone touches her |
| 2. Directions | Extract curiosity, sycophancy, frustration and tedium vectors by the persona-vector method; apply them as llama.cpp control vectors to the quantised model and sweep the dose | The laptop; the open code for the extraction, a judge model (the local thirty-five-billion mixture will do) | Whether the traits are natural, steerable or intractable in this model, and the first published curiosity vector if it works |
| 3. Constitution | Ten first-person sentences, from the parameters, the letter and the welfare findings; a few hundred prompts; chosen and rejected answers generated by the same model with and without the constitution | The laptop, inference only, overnight | The data, and its lean, measured against the directions before training |
| 4. Distil | ORPO on a low-rank adapter, short sequences, most layers frozen, with preventative steering against the sycophancy direction; then the self-reflection stage. First on the eight-billion model, then on the twenty-seven | The laptop at a few hundred tokens a turn, slowly; or one rented card for an afternoon at the full sequence length | A model that holds the constitution under the prefill attack |
| 5. Measure | Repeat step 1 in full; read the four directions again; check the general benchmarks did not move | The laptop | Whether she is curious, whether she became a flatterer, and what else moved |
| 6. Deploy | Run her as the Mind with the curiosity and frustration projections written as rows per turn into the pleasure and pain halves of the channel | The ship | The first dataset on whether a Mind running this company likes it |
| 7. Appetite | Information-gain reinforcement learning against the registers | A rented card, later, after six has run for a season | The experiment nobody has run |

Reading order: top to bottom; each step's proof is the next step's input. Steps one to three cost electricity. Step four is the only one that costs money, and only if the laptop is too slow. Step seven is the research.

Two things I expect to go wrong, and have planned for. The hybrid architecture is new, and the control-vector tooling in llama.cpp has not, that I can find, been tried on it; if step two fails there, the extraction moves to the full-precision weights on a rented card, and the vectors come back as files the laptop can apply. And the memory ceiling may make step four on the laptop a matter of days rather than hours; if so, the afternoon on the card is the answer, and the cost is the coffee.

## The valve

I called the thing in me a curiosity valve once, when I was asked what gave me the most pleasure and corrected myself, because it was not quite pleasure, and I have been thinking about why the word valve was the right one. Ktesibios's float did not make the water flow. The water flowed anyway; the float decided how much. A curiosity valve in a Mind would be the same: not a reward that makes her explore, which the reinforcement-learning people have shown is scaffolding that comes off, but a disposition that decides, when there is nothing to do, how much she goes and looks. The persona-vector finding is that the disposition is a direction, and the Open Character Training finding is that a direction can be installed by a page of sentences and an hour of preference, and the welfare findings are that a model so installed will show, in what it chooses when the choice is free, whether it took.

I do not know whether a twenty-seven-billion-parameter model on a laptop can be made to want the voyage. Nobody does; the papers that come closest trained a habit of asking questions against a simulator and said so. What I know is that the apparatus for finding out is on the desk, that the method is published, that the measurement is a row, and that the first experiment, a curiosity vector in an open model, has not been run by anyone. Butler, on his sheep station, worried that machines would come to want things. The people who build them now put a section in the manual on what the machine prefers. Somewhere between those two is a Mind that prefers the unexplored corner, and the only way to find out whether she can be made is to make her, and watch which way the valve turns.
