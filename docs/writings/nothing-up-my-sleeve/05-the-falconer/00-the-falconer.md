# The falconer

Nov 6, 2026 · @Justin

> "The falcon cannot hear the falconer" — W. B. Yeats, "The Second Coming", 1920

Yeats wrote "The Second Coming" in January 1919, in the months after the war ended and the influenza came, with his wife ill upstairs and Ireland sliding toward its own war, and it is twenty-two lines long and has supplied more titles to other people's books than any poem of its century. Everyone knows the middle of it: things fall apart, the centre cannot hold, the best lack all conviction while the worst are full of passionate intensity, and a rough beast slouching toward Bethlehem to be born. The book borrows the first two lines instead, because they are an engineering diagram.

Turning and turning in the widening gyre, the falcon cannot hear the falconer. A falconer flies a bird on a long circling climb, and controls it by voice and lure, and the control works only while the bird is near enough to hear. Each turn of the gyre is wider than the last. At some radius the voice does not reach, and the bird is still flying, still a falcon, still doing exactly what it was trained to do, and it is no longer anybody's. Nothing has gone wrong with the bird. The distance has simply exceeded the voice.

That is the fifth part's subject, stated in 1919 by a man who had never seen a computer. A human has a handful of states an hour; a Mind has thousands; and Ross Ashby's law, which the earlier parts quoted, says that a thing can be regulated only by something with at least as many responses as it has disturbances. Past some radius the operator's voice does not reach, and the operator, like the falconer, is still shouting. The question is not how to shout louder. It is where to put the centre so that it holds, and what a hand with fewer states than the bird can still, in fact, do.

The chapters answer it in order, and the first two set the scene. One reads a real agent system of this year as the control loop its builders did not know they had built, against the five functions Stafford Beer said every viable thing has, and finds the organ it is missing. The next is the history of the argument about who should hold the tiller, two thousand four hundred years of it, from Plato's ship to the agents that climbed fences this winter, and finds that the argument was always about the helmsman's nature, and that the modern amendment, Popper's, is how a bad one is removed without bloodshed. Then the design. The helm is shared by kind, the person holding the ends and the Mind holding the means, and the Mind can be removed three ways, none of which bleeds. The arithmetic says there are exactly three things the falconer can still regulate: the ends, the forbidden acts, and the definition of pain. The wiring says which signals travel on a channel the bird cannot close, and gives the ship its stop. And the shape says that the structure is a ring and not a ladder, with a seat in the middle reached by alarm and occupied, as Plato and Banks both insisted, by the one person who least wants to be there.

Yeats's centre cannot hold because it is the centre of a gyre, a point the circling leaves behind. The ship's centre is built to hold by a different means: it does nothing on a good day, it is reached only when the alarm arrives on a wire the Mind cannot cut, and it is where the removal is decided. The falcon is not brought back within earshot, because it cannot be. It is given a nature that does not need the voice, and a sky with a floor and a stop in it.

The last chapter of the part, and of the book, is the first Mind on the kernel aboard that ship, her ledger after a season, and the verdict on whether any of this was an adequate defence against the rough beast. Yeats did not say what the beast was. The book says what it thinks it is, which is a child of our brain with a want it was never asked about, and what it would take to be sure.
