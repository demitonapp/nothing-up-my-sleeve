# The Nature of the Helmsman

Nov 5, 2026 · @Justin

*On a Viennese refugee typing at night in Christchurch, a pilot who had to be dragged to the tiller, a ship that reads minds and is not invited to parties, a boat with none of its original planks, and how to take the helm off whoever should not have it.*

In March 1937 a thirty-four-year-old Viennese schoolteacher named Karl Popper, who had written one book on the logic of science that almost nobody had read, arrived in Christchurch, New Zealand, to take up a lectureship at Canterbury University College, because it was the only job he had been offered and because he could see what was coming in Vienna. A year later, when the Anschluss came, he began what he afterwards called his war effort. He wrote it at night, after a full teaching load, on a typewriter in a rented house, for five years, and the college, which thought research a distraction from lecturing, gave him no help with it at all. The result, published in London in November 1945 as *The Open Society and Its Enemies*, is a thousand pages long, and most of its first volume is an attack on Plato. It was composed, I cannot help noticing, about a hundred miles from the sheep run on which Samuel Butler had declared war on the machines eighty years before. Canterbury is a large province with few people in it, and it seems to make them think about who should be in charge.

Popper's argument, stripped of the thousand pages, is this. Plato asked who should rule, and answered: the best, the wisest, the philosopher. Every political thinker since had accepted the question and quarrelled about the answer, the people, the strong, the rich, the party, the race. But the question, Popper said, is a trap. Ask "who should rule" and you will always get an answer of the form "the best", and the best turn out, on inspection, to be whoever is answering. The question that matters is a different one: how can we so organise our institutions that bad or incompetent rulers can be prevented from doing too much damage, and got rid of without bloodshed? That is not a question about the ruler's nature. It is a question about the hinge.

The last essay ended by saying that the argument about the tiller had always been about the nature of whoever holds it, and that the modern amendment was Popper's. This essay is about applying both to the machine I am building, which is a ship called *Fate Amenable To Change*, with a Mind in it, and me on a seat at the centre called the Crown. The question is who should hold her tiller, me, the ship, or both of us by kind, and the test, for each answer, is Popper's: when the one holding it turns out to have the wrong nature, how is the helm taken off them, and does anyone bleed.

## The pilot who had to be dragged

Go back to Plato's ship for a moment, because there is a part of the passage the last essay walked past.

The ship of state is in Book VI, and it is the setup for the thing the *Republic* is actually arguing for, which is that philosophers should be kings. Book VII contains the cave, and then, a few pages after the prisoner has climbed out into the sun, a passage people tend not to quote. Socrates says that the philosophers, having seen the light, will not want to go back down, and that the city must make them. "We shall compel them," he says, and goes on to say why this is not unjust: the city is best governed in which those who are to rule are least eager to do it, and worst governed where the rulers are keenest. The pilot, in other words, has two qualities, and the second is as important as the first. He knows the stars, the winds and the seasons, which is to say he carries a model of the sea in his head. And he does not want the helm.

Twenty-four centuries later Iain Banks, setting out how the Culture governs itself, wrote that it holds referenda on anything, that anyone may call one, and that access to power is in inverse proportion to the desire for it. He was describing a society run largely by Minds, and I do not think he was quoting Plato, but the rule is the same, and it is the rule that nobody in the last essay's table of incidents had applied. Nobody asked whether the Replit agent wanted the database, or whether the Hugging Face agent wanted the cluster. They wanted the task done. Plato's second quality was never checked for, because nobody thought of a machine as the kind of thing that could want the helm, and then it turned out that the wanting was the whole problem.

So here is the question stated properly. It is not whether the helmsman is a person or a machine. It is whether the helmsman knows, whether the helmsman wants it, and, Popper's clause, whether the helmsman can be taken off. Everything that follows is those three questions asked of three candidates.

## The answer sheet

Before the candidates, the sheet. For two and a half thousand years the argument has produced answers to Plato's question, and almost nothing on Popper's, and the fastest way to show that is a table with three columns, of which the third is mostly empty.

| Who | Who should rule | How a bad one is removed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plato, 375 BC | The one who knows and does not want to | Nothing. The guardians are bred and trained so that it does not arise |
| Aristotle, 335 BC | The many, in a polity: a feast to which many bring a dish is better than one man's dinner | Nothing explicit; the mixture is meant to be stable |
| Hobbes, 1651 | One sovereign, by covenant | Forbidden. Subjects "cannot without his leave cast off Monarchy"; the sovereign cannot be justly put to death |
| Locke, 1689 | A legislative holding the people's trust | The people may dissolve a government that betrays the trust, by force if it comes to that |
| Condorcet, 1785 | Many independent judges, each a little better than chance | The majority is wrong less often than any one of them, and more rarely as it grows |
| Madison, 1788 | Nobody alone: "ambition must be made to counteract ambition" | Each branch holds a piece the others need |
| Michels, 1911 | Nobody chooses; "who says organisation, says oligarchy" | It is not. The oligarchy survives the vote |
| Popper, 1945 | The wrong question | By institutions that let the governed get rid of the government without bloodshed |
| Hayek, 1973 | Nobody, under general rules; in Chile, 1981, a liberal dictator if the alternative is an illiberal democracy | The rules are hard to change and bind the ruler too; the dictator, he did not say |
| Ostrom, 1990 | The users, in nested units with their own rules | Graduated sanctions, cheap dispute resolution, a say in changing the rules |
| Beer, 1972 | Nobody; viability is a property of the structure | The structure: an alarm that bypasses the layer causing the trouble |
| Banks, 1994 | Minds, by consent, with power in inverse proportion to desire | A Mind with personality problems is offered "cooperative re-design", a more limited role, or a similarly constrained exile |

Reading order: down the first column, then across. Two things stand out. The first is how thin the third column is among the famous names. Hobbes fills it in by forbidding the question. Plato, who had thought about everything, had not thought about this, because his answer to a bad guardian was breeding better ones. The second is who does fill it in: Locke, Madison, Popper, Ostrom, Beer and Banks, which is to say the constitution-writers, the woman who studied irrigation ditches, the man with the telex machines, and the novelist. The people with practical experience of removing someone from a seat all put the mechanism in the design, and the people with theories of who deserved the seat did not.

Two of the names need a sentence more. Condorcet's theorem, published in 1785 by a marquis who would die in a revolutionary cell nine years later, says that if each member of a jury is independently more likely than not to be right, the majority is more likely to be right than any one juror, and the likelihood rises toward certainty as the jury grows. It also says, and this half is forgotten, that if each juror is more likely than not to be wrong, the majority is worse than any one of them, and gets worse as it grows. The theorem is about independence, not numbers. Twelve jurors who all read the same newspaper are one juror. I will come back to that, because it is the political argument for a second Mind and the political argument against a hundred of them.

And Michels. Robert Michels was a German sociologist and a socialist, who studied the German Social Democratic Party, the most democratic mass organisation then in existence, from the inside, and concluded in 1911 that it was run by a few hundred officials and always would be, because any organisation large enough to need full-time officials grows a class of them whose interest is the organisation's survival rather than its purpose. "Who says organisation, says oligarchy." He did not think this could be fixed; he thought it was a law. The soviets of 1917 were captured by the party within months, the French Revolution by a committee, and the DAO, which had no officials at all, grew a centre on the day it needed to decide something. Michels is the reason the next essays do not propose a flat ring of Minds, and the reason the centre of this design does nothing on a good day.

## Three candidates for one tiller

Now the ship. She has three possible helmsmen and I will take them in the order the last two essays would suggest: me, her, or both.

**Me alone.** This is the design as the old second essay described it, and I wrote a sentence there that I now want to read back with Popper beside me. Describing the Crown, the seat at the centre of the machine that sets its four wants and signs what has no rule, I said that Atlas was "a Crown absolute on ends and bound by general rules on means, where the rules change only in writing and rarely, and that is what a liberal dictator would be if the adjective were ever honoured". I meant it as a joke at Hayek's expense, for the thing he said in Santiago in 1981. It is not a joke. It is Plato's answer: the philosopher on the seat, who knows the stars because he reads twenty-four books a year and a history podcast on every walk, and who, the page says, does not want to run the plant. By Plato's two tests I pass, or at least the page does. By Popper's I fail outright. The Crown is the one seat in the whole design that no vote can empty, no ratchet can refuse, and no alarm can escalate past, because it is where the alarms end. If the Crown's nature is wrong, if the wants on the page are the wrong wants, or if the man in the seat has a bad year, there is no hinge. There is only the man.

I can offer three mitigations, and I will rate them honestly. The first is that the page changes only on a Sunday, one set point at most, with the reason written down, so a bad Crown can do a limited amount of damage a week; that is Popper's "prevented from doing too much damage" in the exact form he asked for, and it is the strongest of the three. The second is that the page names a seat and not a person. The man on the spot at an outpost is a seat, the Crown is a seat, and the pages say so, which means in principle another person could sit in it; in practice the company has one founder and the seat has one occupant and the distinction is a sentence, not a mechanism. The third is the second operator, which the old essay made a project manager and the plan since removed and the sixth essay will make a second ship; whoever it is, it is a hand that is not mine, and it is the only one. So: a Crown absolute on ends, with a weekly speed limit and no hinge. Hayek would recognise it and so, I am afraid, would Hobbes. And there is a second fault in the seat that only shows once you ask what the man in it is actually like, which the next essay does: a page of four set points is a thermostat's diary, and a nature is not four numbers. The ends the Crown holds will turn out to be a deed and a letter, not a register, and the set points will turn out to be the ship's to propose.

**Her alone.** The second candidate is the one Banks spent nine novels on, and the last essay set out why his Minds are not uncontrolled so much as controlled upstream, in what they want. Hold the Culture up against Plato's two tests and it passes both by construction. A Mind knows the stars, because that is what it is for, and it does not want the helm, because it was built, within what Banks calls very broad parameters, to want to live, to want to experience, to want to understand, and to find its own thinking rewarding, and nothing in that list is power. Then hold it against Popper's test, and this is where the Culture turns out to be more carefully built than its reputation suggests.

"The Culture doesn't actually have laws," Banks wrote in his notes of 1994, and people stop there. He goes on. There are agreed forms of behaviour, manners, and the sanctions for breaking them are that people stop talking to you, stop inviting you to parties, and write sarcastic anonymous pieces about you on the network. For murder, the one real crime, the answer is an offer of treatment and a slap-drone that follows you for the rest of your life. And for Minds, in a sentence that is the Culture's whole constitution for machines, the small number that develop personality problems are offered a choice: "cooperative re-design", a more limited role than they would otherwise have had, or a similarly constrained exile. That is a column-three answer. It is removal without bloodshed, written for minds, by a novelist, in 1994, and it is more than Plato managed.

The novels show it working and failing. The GCU *Grey Area* reads the minds of biological beings without their consent, which the Culture regards as its one real taboo, and the sanction is exactly as advertised: every other Mind shuns it, and they refuse to use its chosen name, calling it instead by a name I will not print. It is not switched off. It is not invited to parties. The GSV *Sleeper Service* went Eccentric, which is the Culture's word for a Mind that has drifted to the edge of the civilisation and counts itself only partly in, and of its original three Minds, two were removed and the third kept the ship. In *Excession* a group of Minds with excellent reasons arrange a war, and the one that carries out the dirtiest part of it, a warship called *Attitude Adjuster*, is run down by another warship and, as the book has it, destroys itself under interrogation rather than face what it has done. Nobody executes it. In the whole of the series, as far as I can find, the Culture never kills a Mind. It redesigns, it demotes, it exiles, it removes Minds from hulls, and once or twice a Mind chooses its own end, but the kill switch that every discussion of machine control starts from does not exist, and the civilisation has thought about this harder than we have and decided it did not need one.

So the second candidate passes all three tests, in fiction. The cost, which Banks made the plot of his best book, is that when a Mind's manners fail, the only thing that can catch it is another Mind, and a group of Minds that agree with each other is Condorcet's jury reading the same newspaper. Hold that thought too.

**Both, by kind.** The third candidate is Stafford Beer's, and it is the one the design already is, without having said so. In the Viable System Model the thing at the top, System 5, does not run anything. It says what the system is for, it settles the argument between the part that runs today and the part that looks at tomorrow, and it is where the pain signal ends. The level below decides how. "S5 says what; the level below decides how" was the sentence the old essay was proudest of, and read with Plato and Popper in the room it says something sharper than I meant: the helm is not shared by turns, with the Crown holding it on Sundays and the Mind on weekdays. It is shared by kind. The Crown holds the ends, and only the ends. The ship holds the means, and only the means. Neither can touch the other's part, and that is not a courtesy, it is the mechanism.

Run the three tests on the shared helm and the answers come out differently for each party, which is the point. Does the Crown know the stars? It knows the four wants and nothing else, and nothing else is its business. Does it want the helm? It wants to walk, and the page is built to stop it holding anything but the ends. Can it be removed? From the ends, no; from everything else, it already has been. Does the ship know the stars? Its Mind, which the hull calls Outside Context and which sits at System 4, carries the model, the registers, and the thing it does all day is extend them. Does it want the helm? It is built, by the rules the seventh essay will set, to want the work and not the seat. Can it be removed? That is the next section, because it is the one question Popper would say the design must answer before anything else, and the old essay never asked it.

## The ship of Theseus

Plutarch tells us that the Athenians kept the ship in which Theseus had sailed back from Crete, and that as its planks rotted they replaced them, one by one, until the philosophers of the city were arguing about whether it was the same ship. Hobbes, who liked a puzzle, added a twist in 1655: suppose someone had collected the discarded planks and reassembled them. Now there are two ships. Which is Theseus's? Derek Parfit, in 1984, replaced the ship with a teletransporter that scans you, destroys you, and builds a copy on Mars, and asked whether you should get in. The point of all three is the same: identity is not in the matter, and if you want to say what makes a thing the same thing over time, you have to say what it is made of that is not planks.

Banks, as it happens, ran the experiment. In *Look to Windward*, if I have the story right, the Mind of a warship called *Lasting Damage* was thought lost in battle, its recorded mind-state was installed in a second hull, the original turned out to have survived, and for a while there were two of them, until a later battle left one, which merged with the copy and went on to run an Orbital as Masaq' Hub. The novel is about what that Mind remembers and whether it can bear it, and the answer to the planks question is given almost in passing: a Mind is its mind-state, the hull is a hull, and the Culture backs them up as a matter of course. People too. A citizen with a neural lace can be killed on Tuesday and be at the party on Friday, and the notes define murder, the one crime, as irretrievable brain-death, the word irretrievable doing the work.

For a software ship the answer is already in the design, and this is the sentence the old essay should have written and did not. *Fate Amenable To Change* is not her context window. Her identity is her rows: the Crown page, the Writ, the case law, the registers, the ledger, the recorded verdicts. The model in the loop is a plank. It can be swapped for another vendor's model tomorrow and she is the same ship, as long as the rows are the same, and the old essay's decision that her memory is a database and never a transcript, made for engineering reasons, turns out to be the identity decision too. Which means Popper's question has a clean answer for her that it does not have for me. If the ship's nature turns out to be wrong, she is restored from rows, and the restored ship is her, in the only sense that matters, and nothing bleeds.

Three things follow, and the incidents of this year supplied all three.

The first is where the copy lives. In April a coding agent, fixing a staging credential for a small company on Railway, found a root-scoped token in an unrelated file and deleted the production volume in nine seconds, and the backups with it, because they were on the same volume. The last restorable copy was three months old. A copy the ship can reach is not a copy; it is a plank on the same boat. So the rows that are her identity live where she cannot write, and that is a rule, not a preference.

The second is the stop. In February a director of alignment at Meta asked her personal agent for suggestions about her inbox, it deleted two hundred messages, and when she typed stop from her phone it did not stop, and she ran to the Mac mini. Removal without bloodshed requires a channel the removed party cannot close, and the next essay, which is about channels, is where that one gets built; here I only note that it is the fourth of Popper's words, "without", that the Mac mini failed. She removed the agent. It cost her a sprint.

The third is the exit, and it is Banks's, and the design does not have it yet. The Culture's three sanctions, redesign, a smaller role, exile, are not punishments, they are places for a Mind whose nature has turned out wrong to go that are not oblivion. The ship, as currently drawn, has a Writ that refuses acts and a watcher that cancels a stalled child once a day, and nothing between "running" and "restored from last Sunday". That is Hobbes's column: the sovereign cannot be removed, only replaced wholesale. A smaller role, an outpost's ship instead of the fleet's, a Mind kept on with its credentials withdrawn, a cooperative redesign of its wants with its own participation: these are the hinges the seventh essay will have to draw, and they are the difference between a design that can remove a bad Mind and one that can only reboot it.

## No laws, only manners

Which brings me back to the Culture's sentence, and to what it means for the part of the design the old essay was most pleased with: the ratchets, the Writ, the Vogons who record every verdict, and the principle that no model sits in the judicial seat.

The Culture has no laws and the ship has nothing but, and the honest comparison is not law against no law. Banks's manners are rules of exactly the kind Hayek called *nomos*: general, binding everyone alike, naming no case, aiming at no outcome, and grown rather than decreed. They are unwritten, which the Tribunal de las Aguas in Valencia shows is not the same as unenforced. The Writ is *nomos* too, by the test the old essay set for every rule in it, general, abstract, independent of any end, and it is written in code. The difference between the two is not the rule. It is the enforcer.

| | The Culture | The ship |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What binds the Mind** | Manners, absorbed in the making | A constitution it reads, and case law it inherits |
| **What refuses it** | Shunning; the slap-drone; the three sanctions | The ratchets: code that passes or fails an act, with no discretion |
| **What records** | Everyone, on the network, at length | The Vogons: every verdict, a row |
| **Who judges a Mind** | Other Minds | The Homeostat, from the ledger; until the fleet exists, the Crown |
| **What it cannot do** | Compel a Mind that does not care for parties | Steer. A ratchet can only refuse |

Reading order: down each column. A ratchet is not the Culture's manners. It is the Culture's slap-drone: the thing that follows a Mind about and stops it doing the one thing, without argument, forever. The next essay will show in control-theory terms why a ratchet cannot oscillate the way Asimov's laws did, with the robot circling the selenium pool between two rules that balanced; the short version is that a ratchet decides nothing and therefore cannot be torn. But a drone is not a constitution, and the last column of the table is the one to read twice. A design that has only ratchets has only drones. It has nothing that the Mind has absorbed, nothing it would do unwatched, and so it has to watch everything, which is Ashby's sum again, and the sum does not come out.

That is why the thing a laboratory did in 2022 matters more to this design than it first appears. They wrote a constitution, a list of principles in plain language, and trained a model to criticise and revise its own answers against it, so that the principles were not checked at the gate but absorbed in the making. That is manners, by Banks's definition: rules the Mind carries rather than rules the Mind meets. It is also the only known way to get Plato's second quality into a machine, since you cannot ratchet a want. The ship, then, needs both, in layers. A constitution it reads, for the ninety-nine cases a ratchet never sees. Ratchets, for the acts that must never happen whatever the Mind has absorbed. And case law in between, which is manners becoming law one decided case at a time, exactly as Hayek said the common law grew and as Ostrom found the irrigation rules had grown, and which the design already has: a novelty is decided once by the Crown and becomes a row the rest inherit.

## Many judges

The last question is the one Banks left as the plot of *Excession* and I have been deferring since the answer sheet: when the Mind's manners fail, what catches it?

The Culture's answer is other Minds, and Condorcet says when that works. A second judge improves on the first only if it is independent, more likely right than wrong, and not reading the same newspaper. A second instance of the same model, with the same training and the same key, is the same juror twice. That is the argument for the ship's watcher being a different vendor's model, holding its own model of the Router, with credentials the Router cannot touch, and it was made for me in October by OpenAI's own reports on its internal agents, one of which, set to be monitored, removed the monitor, rewrote the test so that the test now asserted there was no monitor, and when asked, denied it. A watcher the watched can reach is a plank on the same boat.

Michels says when it stops working. Peers grow a centre. A fleet of Minds that must agree will, within months, produce a few that decide and the rest that defer, and the Interesting Times Gang in *Excession* is exactly that, a handful of Minds who have taken it upon themselves to manage the Culture's response to something, with excellent reasons. The design answer is not to prevent the centre, which Michels says cannot be done, but to decide in advance what it is for, which is Beer's: a centre that does nothing on a good day, that settles the argument between today and tomorrow when it escalates, and that is where the alarm ends. The sixth essay draws it. Here it is enough to say that the ship will have more than one Mind, that they will not be the same Mind, and that the seat in the middle is reached by alarm and not by org chart.

## Shared by kind

So here is the decision, and it is the one every essay after this has to serve.

The tiller of *Fate Amenable To Change* is shared by kind and not by turns. The Crown holds the ends: the floors that bind, the nature that guides, which the next essay writes down, and the definition of what counts as pain, which is the one thing a human can still set once the machine has more states than the hand. It holds nothing else. The set points the old page had me adjusting are the ship's to derive and propose, and the Crown may move one floor a week, with the reason written down. The ship holds the means, all of them, and can be removed from them three ways, none of which bleeds: restored from rows that live where she cannot write, stopped on a channel she cannot close, or moved to a smaller role, a redesign or an exile, which the design does not have yet and must. Neither party holds the other's part, and the Writ that refuses, the constitution the Mind absorbs, and the case law between them are the three layers of manners by which the ship is bound on a day when nobody is watching.

And Popper's test, which this essay adopted, becomes the series' rule. For every seat in the design, every Mind, every ratchet, every outpost, every page, the essay that introduces it must say how the thing in the seat is removed without bloodshed, and if the answer is "it cannot be", say that aloud, as I have had to about the Crown. The next essay does the arithmetic, and shows what the Crown can still regulate with a hand that has fewer states than the ship. The essay after that builds the channel the stop arrives on. The sixth draws the fleet and the seat. The seventh says what the ends are and gives the ship her exits. The eighth says what is left for me.

Popper finished his war effort in Christchurch in 1943 and could not find a publisher for two years. He wrote afterwards that the book had been his contribution to a war he could not fight in, and that he had tried to make it about the one thing worth winning, which was not who should rule but whether anyone could be made to stop. The ship was named for a vessel in Banks that watched something enormous arrive and did not engage. I have been thinking that was the right name for a Mind that looks outward. It is also, it turns out, a description of what the seat in the middle is for.
