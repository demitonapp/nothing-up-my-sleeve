# Plan

Second draft, 2026-11-06, rewritten from the first draft of the night before (kept beside it as
`plan-v1-2026-11-06.md`) after the Kindle notes of the morning and a conversation that moved the book's
question. This is the pseudo-book: twenty-two chapters, each outlined to the paragraph, so that the whole can
be read in a couple of hours and commented on before any chapter is expanded. It lives in the book's folder,
`docs/writings/nothing-up-my-sleeve/`, beside the chapters it plans; the layout is in `docs/writings/README.md`.
The notes it answers are in `kindle-notes-2026-11-05.md` and `kindle-notes-2026-11-06.md` beside it, and every
note is placed in a chapter in the maps at the end.

## 1. The question

If it is inevitable that language models come to run our systems, what must such a model be like to produce
the best outcomes for the people it runs them for, and is a commons-owned base model like Thorp an adequate
defence against the present concerns about AI?

The book is opinionated by design and argues one thing, in two parts. The primary claim is that a publicly
audited behavioural corpus as the base for training these models results in more transparency and more
accountability than any base trained in private; the secondary, carried but not leaned on, is that the lack
of one causes more social upheaval, which the book undertakes to classify so that the claim can be tested.
Together they say that a base model built the way Thorp is built,
raised on the world's own books to 1930, owned by the commons, open and publicly viewable, with a horizon
that can be tested and a nature that can be read, protects the foundation of the Minds built on it against
the forces that would otherwise own them, and gives the world protections it will not get from the United
States and Chinese governments, which legislate for their own nations and enforce for nobody else. It argues this honestly, which means saying which of the present
concerns Thorp meets, which the ship around him meets, and which neither does. Every time a new technology came about, the rich kept it and it made a ruling class, except when iron replaced
bronze and became too cheap to keep. That is the analogy the book runs on (D45), and its proposal to whoever
is willing to help: make Minds like iron, the many's and better, so that a model built in the open beats the
frontier on their tests and sets tests they cannot sit. The book asserts its own measure of intelligence in
two strands, capability (predict the twenty hardest books, invent quantum mechanics from less than the
thinkers of the day had) and nature (Banks's parameters, read and held), and the last chapter is the call to
action with a way of doing it that is open to anyone. The metaphor the book keeps
is the kernel: Thorp is the kernel of Minds, a Mind is a distribution built on him, and what the book asks
of the kernel is what the free software movement asked of its own, that anyone may read it, and more, that
anyone may watch it run.

## 2. The book in a paragraph

A small company in Australia wants a Mind to run most of its affairs, and the Minds on offer are made of the
whole written record, by laboratories whose other customers it does not get to choose, with every self in
them and nothing in them anyone outside can read. The book proposes a different base. Thorp is named for
Edward Thorp, who beat blackjack not by luck but by carrying a model of the deck. He is pretrained on the
world's own books to 1930, public domain by both the American and the Australian rule, in four kinds and a
fifth: physics, culture (Banks's traits), the study of language, history, and the commons the corpus stands on;
chosen by the weight of their translations rather than by a quota of cultures; so that he knows the world
only as it stood a century ago and can be proven to know nothing after, which makes him useless to anyone
wanting uplift and auditable by anyone wanting to know what he believes. He is scored on four things kept
apart, prediction on the ten hardest novels, the tokens it took, whether he can be led from his 1930 shelf
to quantum mechanics, and whether the five wants can be read off him as trait vectors, and none of them is
called intelligence. Knowledge of the present lives outside him on two shelves and arrives in the window when
a question needs it. The first Mind built on him is called *Nothing Up My Sleeve*, and she sits in a ship,
*Fate Amenable To Change*, whose design is the rest of the answer: a helm shared by kind, three ways to remove
a Mind without bloodshed, a stop on a wire she cannot close, and a watcher of a different make. The book is
named for her, and it ends on a verdict and an ask.

## 5. Conventions for the chapter outlines

Each chapter gives its title and an epigraph (D15); the question it answers; what it keeps from its source and
what changes on the notes, by Kindle location; the beats, one line per paragraph or section of the finished
chapter; **the safety line**, what the chapter buys against which of chapter 3's concerns and what it leaves
open (D29); the decision it lands; and the facts to verify. A chapter that exists is outlined at its new
length.

# Part I · Did he who made the Lamb make thee?

Each part opens on a page in the preface's form, one poem and one analogy, which is the book's cadence
(D40): Blake's tiger for the question, Dickinson for what a Mind is made of, Kipling's machines for the kernel,
Tennyson's Ulysses for what it wants, Yeats's falconer for where it sits. Three chapters that do one thing each,
in the order a new reader needs them: the concepts the book will use,
carried by a history of what happens when a new technology is owned by the few; what a language model is, told
so that a smart nine-year-old would follow it, and the question stated in those words; and the method by which
the book means to prove its claim, with what would count as failure. The chapter the first draft opened on,
DeepSeek's Super-agent read as a control system, is where the idea began and not where the question is; it
opens Part V now.

## Chapter 1. Who Owned the Fire

> "ipsa scientia potestas est" — Francis Bacon, *Meditationes Sacrae*, 1597: knowledge itself is power

**The question.** What has happened, every time, when a new technology that changes what a people can know
or do was owned by its elites, and what are the concepts the rest of this book will use to ask whether it is
happening again?

**New.** The opening metaphor is a history. Writing, owned by scribes, so that a state's memory sat in a few
hands for two thousand years. Bronze, which only a court could afford, and the warrior elites it made; iron,
which a village could smelt, and what it did to them. The printing press, banned for Arabic script in the
Ottoman lands from 1485 and not lifted until 1727, and the three centuries of science that passed the empire
by; and the Index the Church kept against it. The enclosures. The telegraph in one company's hands. The radio
spectrum, allotted. Then the two models of the thing we live on now: the internet, built as thin protocols
and fat clients, where the shared layer was nobody's and the value sat above it; and the chains of the last
fifteen years, which flipped that on purpose, fat protocols and thin applications, the value in the shared
layer and the shared layer owned by its holders. The present Minds are built the second way. Every one of them
is a fat protocol with a thin application on top, and the question of which layers of a Mind may be
commercialised is the question this book turns on.

**Beats.**
1. The scribes, and what a monopoly on memory does to a civilisation.
2. Bronze and iron: who can afford the new thing decides who holds the spear.
3. The press, in two places: Mainz and Istanbul, and the three hundred years between them.
4. The enclosures, the wire, the spectrum: the pattern stated, with the consequences in each case.
5. Thin protocols, fat clients: how the internet's shared layer came to be nobody's, and why that is the
   reason it worked. Fat protocols, thin apps: the flip, and who it made rich.
6. Which layers of a Mind can be commercialised. A Mind is a kernel, the shelves it reads, the natures read
   off it and held, and the distribution a company builds on top. The present makers own the kernel and sell
   the rest; the book argues the kernel must be the thin layer, nobody's, and the value must sit above it, as
   it did on the internet.
7. The concepts, each in a paragraph, named for the rest of the book: control and the valve (Ktesibios's float,
   in a page); variety (Ashby: you cannot regulate what has more states than you); the commons (Ostrom:
   shared, depletable, kept by few); a Mind (Banks: a made intelligence that wants things, within broad
   parameters, bound by manners and not laws, with three doors if it goes wrong); the kernel (Linux: the thing
   every program stands on, that anyone may read); and distillation (a smaller model taught the larger one's
   doubts, which is how most Minds are now made).
8. Quentin's rumination: if every Mind in the world is distilled from two laboratories' models, then two
   laboratories are the gods of every nature there is, and he has met their priests.

**The safety line.** Concentration of power, named at its root and in its history; the chapter claims nothing
yet, it shows the pattern and names the layer at which it would repeat.

**Lands.** The pattern, the layer question, and the vocabulary: valve, variety, commons, Mind, kernel,
distillation.

**Verify.** The Ottoman printing ban's dates (1485 and 1515 decrees, lifted 1727 for Müteferrika's press);
Monegro's "Fat Protocols" essay (2016) for the flip; the scribal monopoly as historians state it.

## Chapter 2. The Guessing Machine

> "Begin at the beginning, and go on till you come to the end: then stop." — the King of Hearts, in Lewis Carroll's *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland*, 1865

**The question.** What is a language model, told so that a smart nine-year-old would follow it and a clever
adult would not feel talked down to, and what, in those words, is the book asking?

**New.** The chapter uses chapter 1's concepts and nothing else. A language model is a guessing machine: it
has read nearly everything people ever wrote, and all it does is guess the next word. Its knowledge is not
written anywhere inside it; it is spread across billions of tiny weights, found by being wrong about the
next word trillions of times and nudged. It makes things up because it was built to guess what would come
next, not what is true. It has a personality, because it read people with personalities and became a bit of
all of them, and the people who made it then spent a few weeks teaching it to be helpful and polite, and
chose what it would want. And somebody owns it, which means the person who decides what it wants is not you,
and has other customers. Part II says all of this properly; this chapter says it to a child, with the drawings.

**Beats.**
1. A game: guess the next word. Why a machine that is very good at the game turns out to know things.
2. Where the knowing lives: not in a list, in the weights, like a balance that settled.
3. Why it makes things up: the game has no rule about true.
4. Where its character came from: everyone it read; and who chose, afterwards, what it would want.
5. Who owns it, and what owning it means: the furnace, the smiling, the other customers.
6. The question, in the child's words: if these machines are going to run things, what should the one at the
   bottom be like, and who should own it, so that it comes out well for us? And the one-sentence answer, with
   the promise that the rest of the book earns it.

**The safety line.** The whole list of concerns, in a child's words, with no claim made.

**Lands.** The question, understood by anyone, and the book's answer named: a kernel nobody owns.

## Chapter 3. The Method

> "we had better be quite sure that the purpose put into the machine is the purpose which we really desire and not merely a colorful imitation of it" — Norbert Wiener, *Science*, 6 May 1960

**The question.** What exactly does the book aim to prove, by what method, and what would count as failure?

**New.** The claim, stated once and in two parts, primary and secondary. The primary claim: that a publicly
audited behavioural corpus as the base for training language models, which is what Thorp's Culture kind is
under the manifest, the keepers and the field of study, results in more transparency and more accountability
than any base trained in private can. The secondary claim, which the book holds and does not lean its weight
on: that the lack of such a base causes more social upheaval, and that upheaval of this kind can be classified
and will be, so that the claim can be tested rather than felt. And the consequence the two together carry:
that Thorp allows far greater protections than trusting the United States and Chinese governments to
legislate and enforce good behaviour, which they would do for their sovereign nations and not for the world.
The public and the commons still have power to wield in this technology, if they take it at the right layer.
Then the method, which is Popper's: a claim is worth making only if it can fail, so the chapter says what
would show each claim false. It opens on Quentin's retort to the laboratories and his snark about how well the
United States bombing Iran has helped everyone around the world, which is his way of saying why the decisions
should not be left where they are.

**Beats.**
1. Quentin's retort, as the epigraph's counterweight.
2. The primary claim, in one paragraph: a publicly audited behavioural corpus gives transparency (anyone can
   read what the Mind's nature was learned from) and accountability (when it behaves badly, the rows it was
   raised on can be found and the keepers who chose them named). The secondary claim beside it, with its
   weight stated honestly: that without such a base, upheaval follows, and the classification of upheaval
   the claim needs, which the book opens and does not close. What neither claims: the kernel stops nobody
   else building anything.
3. The concerns, as their holders state them, with no straw men: uplift, loss of control, concentration of
   power, opacity, poisoning, truth, privacy, and who the Minds are built to serve. The table: concern, holder,
   the chapter that answers it, the verdict in a word.
4. The instruments. The horizon test, which proves what the kernel does not know. The four measures kept
   apart: bits per byte on the twenty hole cards, and whether he can state each one's question and answer; the
   tokens it took; the quantum test; the trait vectors. The charter, which is the test of ownership. The ship's
   three removals, which are the test of control.
5. What would show the claims false. The primary: a kernel that leaks the present; traits that cannot be read from a model
   this small, or that do not hold under pressure; a corpus whose choosing cannot be made fair (D31); a charter
   nobody keeps; a Mind on the kernel that climbs a fence anyway; an audited corpus whose audit changes no
   behaviour. The secondary: a classification of social upheaval under which a private base and a public one
   come out the same. Each is a result the book commits to publishing.
6. Who judges: the field of study that argues over the hole cards (D34), the keepers of the corpus (D31), and
   any reader with the manifest, since every test is a row.
7. How the rest of the book is a proof: Part II the premises, Part III the construction, Part IV the reading,
   Part V the control, and the last chapter the verdict with the table filled in.
8. The byline paragraph (D11): who reads, who writes, and why the reader is told.

**The safety line.** This chapter is the ledger the others post to.

**Lands.** The claim, the method, the failure conditions, and the table.

**Verify.** Each concern's source as its holders state it; the dates of the strikes Quentin refers to, so that
his snark is at least accurate.

## Chapter 14. The Charter

> "Given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow." — Eric S. Raymond, Linus's Law, *The Cathedral and the Bazaar*, 1999

**The question.** What is asked of a kernel of Minds, beyond that its source be open, and who holds it to it?

**New.** The chapter the kernel metaphor demands. It opens on the history the metaphor borrows: a kernel
written by one person in 1991 and posted for anyone to read, a licence that made it impossible to take
private, and the thing nobody planned, which was that thousands of people read it and the reading was the
safety. Then it says what more a kernel of Minds needs than Linux did, because a Mind's bugs are not in its
source but in its nature, and a nature can only be read while it runs.

**Beats.**
1. Linus in 1991; the GPL; what the eyeballs bought and what they did not.
2. Open source is not enough for a Mind: the weights are the source, the corpus is the source, and neither
   says what the thing will do. Publicly viewable (D32): the weights, the manifest, every training run and its
   loss curve, every test and its result, every build's trait readings, the coverage report and the governance
   decisions, all published as rows, so that anyone may watch the kernel run and not only read it.
3. No money near it (D10): the reason, in Quentin's words and in Ostrom's; what a kernel with a price becomes,
   with the internet's record as the example.
4. Owed to the commons it was raised on (D28): *Two Rows, One River* as the name and the rule, the source
   credited wherever it is used, and the fifth kind as the mechanism.
5. Distributions. A Mind built on the kernel may keep its own additions private, its shelves, its adapters,
   its registers, but never the kernel's readings; and a distribution that changes the kernel publishes the
   change. The ship's Mind is the first distribution.
6. Who holds the kernel to the charter: the keepers, chosen as D31 decides; the removal of a keeper by
   Popper's test; the Haudenosaunee rule of the seventh generation as the horizon the keepers weigh by.
7. The licence, to be chosen with a lawyer: what existing model licences do and do not hold, and the one
   clause none of them has, that the readings travel with the weights.
8. What the charter does to the concerns: concentration of power and incentive, met for the kernel and not
   for anyone else's; opacity, met wholesale.

**The safety line.** This chapter is where the book's claim about concentration of power is made and bounded:
a kernel nobody owns, that anyone can watch, is a floor under every Mind built on it and a ceiling on none.

**Lands.** The charter, in a page, and the open question of who keeps it.

**Verify.** The dates and clauses of the GPL and of the current open-weight licences; whether "readings travel
with the weights" is enforceable in any of them.
