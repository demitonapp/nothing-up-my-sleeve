# A Modest Declaration

*On floors, ceilings, and the hazards of sorting things*

In November 2023 the representatives of twenty-eight countries and the European Union gathered at Bletchley Park, a house chiefly famous for having kept a secret for thirty years, and issued a public declaration. It concerned the most powerful AI systems in the world, and its gist was that they might be dangerous and that somebody ought to look into it. Nobody could object to this, which is generally how you tell a declaration from a decision.

What strikes me is where everyone was looking, which was up. The whole document is about the ceiling. Nobody said a word about the floor.

So here is a rival declaration, in two clauses. First, language models should be accessible to all. Second, language ability should be set out across a proper taxonomy (spelling, punctuation, grammar, style, the assembling of a paragraph, and so on upward) and normalised in a base kernel model. That means a small, open, thoroughly known thing on which everything else is built.

It has a distinguished ancestor. In 1792 two French astronomers, Delambre and Méchain, set off to measure the meridian from Dunkirk to Barcelona so that the world could have a metre. France at the time is said to have had a quarter of a million local weights and measures, so a pint in one village was a diplomatic incident in the next. The motto of the new system was "for all time, for all people", which is roughly clause one with better tailoring.

The case in favour is strong. A normalised kernel is one you can account for: you know what it read, what it can do, and at which rung of the ladder it gives up. It separates the ability to parse a sentence from having an opinion about everything on the internet, two faculties we have so far bought only as a set. It runs on a computer the size of a box of chocolates. And it gives science a fixed point, because you cannot discover what a book teaches a model if the model is a different animal every time you look.

Now the difficulties, which are several, and mostly to do with the word "taxonomy".

In 1668 John Wilkins, a founder of the Royal Society who had married Oliver Cromwell's sister and written a book about flying to the moon, published a scheme sorting everything in the universe into forty categories, with a language to match. It was magnificent and nobody ever used it. Borges later compared it to an imaginary Chinese encyclopaedia that divided animals into those belonging to the Emperor and those that from a distance resemble flies.

Two centuries on, Melvil Dewey, a man so devoted to tidiness that he briefly respelled his own surname "Dui", sorted all human knowledge into ten classes. He gave most of religion's ten divisions to Christianity and left every other faith on earth to share the last one. A taxonomy, in short, is a portrait of the taxonomist. Whoever draws the taxonomy of language decides what counts as language ability, and they will draw it, quite innocently, to look like themselves.

"Normalised" also wants watching. Samuel Johnson set out to fix the English language. He promised it in three years and took nine. He then admitted in his preface that trying to enchain syllables was like trying to lash the wind. This was the same man who defined oats as a grain given to horses in England and to people in Scotland, so the standard arrived with opinions attached.

Our own kernel reads only English printed before 1930 by authors safely dead, which gives it the voice of an Edwardian rural dean. On a Portuguese novel it scores 2.77 bits per byte against 1.62 for Dostoevsky in translation. That is accessible to all, provided all are Anglophone and ideally deceased.

There are three further snags.

- **Standards preserve their mistakes.** Méchain found an error in his own measurements, hid it, and fretted about it until he died. The metre has been about a fifth of a millimetre short ever since.
- **The better standard does not always win.** Brunel's broad gauge was by most accounts smoother and faster than Stephenson's four feet eight and a half inches. It lost anyway, and the last of it was ripped up over a single weekend in May 1892. A kernel standardised too early is the narrow gauge with a press release.
- **The floor may simply be low.** C. K. Ogden's Basic English got the language down to 850 words and won Churchill's enthusiasm in 1943. Wags then pointed out that "blood, toil, tears and sweat" came out as something like "blood, work, eye water and face water". Accessibility achieved by subtraction is not quite the same as accessibility.

There is also the examiner's curse: once the taxonomy is the test, models will learn the test, and we shall have kernels that pass beautifully and cannot order lunch.

Still, I would sign it, with three amendments.

1. **Normalise the measuring, not the speaking.** The declaration should fix the ruler and leave the voices alone.
2. **Version the taxonomy and expect it to be wrong.** Do what Méchain could not bring himself to do, and publish the error.
3. **Postpone "all" until there is a second language.** The word should wait until the kernel reads more than English, and preferably a seventh language too.

Bletchley worries, rightly, about how high the ceiling might go. But almost nobody lives on the ceiling. Everyone else is on the floor, and it would be nice if it were level, and if somebody could say what it was made of.
